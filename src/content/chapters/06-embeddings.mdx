## What an embedding is

An embedding is a vector: an ordered list of numbers produced from an input such as a chunk, a query, an image, or a code file.

<RagIllustration kind="vector-encoding" />

The numbers are not a human-readable dictionary of facts. They are coordinates in a learned representation space. A model is trained so that some relationships useful for its training objectives are reflected in geometric relationships between vectors.

For RAG, the important operation is to embed documents and queries into compatible representations, then compare them to find candidate passages. The embedding is a representation of the input, not a guarantee that the input is true, complete, authorized, or relevant to every possible question.

### How embeddings learn similarity

A text encoder first turns text into tokens, then processes them through learned layers. It combines the resulting token representations into a fixed-size vector, sometimes with a learned projection. The exact architecture and pooling step vary by model; the key output is a vector whose coordinates are learned parameters of the encoder, not hand-written scores for words.

The encoder alone does not make nearby vectors mean “similar.” Training supplies that meaning. For a retrieval or similarity objective, examples considered related are trained to score closer than examples considered unrelated—often by comparing positive pairs against negative alternatives. This shapes the space so its geometry is useful for that training task. Sentence-BERT, for example, uses siamese or triplet structures to produce sentence vectors intended to be compared with cosine similarity. [Reimers and Gurevych, *Sentence-BERT*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084)

At inference time, the system encodes new inputs and applies the model's intended comparison metric. Cosine similarity compares direction; dot product can also reflect vector magnitude; Euclidean distance measures separation. A smaller distance or larger similarity score means “closer under this model, training objective, and metric”—not objectively equivalent in meaning, and not proof of relevance or correctness.

### A concrete cross-modal example: CLIP aligns images and text

CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training) makes the idea of a learned comparison space concrete. It has an image encoder and a text encoder that map their different inputs into a shared vector space. During training, it learns to give paired image-caption examples greater similarity than mismatched pairs. The geometry is therefore shaped by the training objective and data; it is not a universal map of meaning. [Radford et al., *Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020); see also [OpenAI's CLIP overview](https://openai.com/index/clip/).

At use time, an image and several candidate descriptions can be encoded, then compared using cosine similarity (or an equivalent dot product when the vectors are normalized). The highest-scoring description is the closest match among those candidates according to CLIP. That score does **not** prove the description is true, nor is it a probability of correctness. It is a model-dependent ranking signal.

The same intuition carries over to text retrieval: a query and passages are mapped into a compatible space, and a metric ranks nearby candidates. But CLIP is a cross-modal image-text model, not evidence that every text embedding model learns the same geometry or that geometric closeness always means task relevance.

### One vector represents many properties

A text chunk can express a topic, entities, intent, language, style, and relationships between concepts. A single vector compresses those properties into a fixed-dimensional representation. That makes fast comparison possible, but it also means that distinctions important to a task may be blurred.

For example, two passages may both discuss database backups while only one specifies a retention period. Their embeddings can be close even though the distinction matters to the question. Retrieval systems often combine semantic retrieval with lexical matching, metadata filters, or reranking to handle these cases.

### Document and query embeddings

At indexing time, each prepared chunk becomes a document vector. At query time, the user question becomes a query vector. The index searches for document vectors close to the query vector according to a chosen metric.

<RagIllustration kind="embedding-flow" />

The query and document encoders may be the same model or related components trained for their respective roles. What matters is compatibility: a query vector must be compared with vectors produced under the assumptions the index expects.

## Similarity and distance

These three views explain common ways a system compares a query vector `q` with a document vector `d`. The geometry is simplified to two dimensions; real embeddings usually have many more.

<RagIllustration kind="similarity-metrics" />

### Cosine similarity

Cosine similarity compares the angle between two vectors, as shown above.

Here `q · d` is the dot product, and `||q||` and `||d||` are vector lengths. The score focuses on direction rather than magnitude. If vectors are normalized to unit length, cosine similarity and dot product produce the same ranking.

### Dot product

The dot product sums pairwise coordinate products. Geometrically, it rewards alignment and, when vectors are not normalized, also reflects magnitude. Whether magnitude should influence ranking is a model and system decision. A system must use the metric for which its embedding model and index configuration were designed.

### Euclidean distance

Euclidean distance measures the straight-line distance between vectors, as shown above.

For normalized vectors, Euclidean distance and cosine similarity are monotonically related, so they produce equivalent rankings when applied consistently. Without normalization, they can rank candidates differently.

### A score is not a probability

Similarity scores are relative geometric measurements. A score of `0.8` does not mean “80% likely to be correct.” Scores from different models, metrics, normalization choices, or datasets should not be compared as if they shared one universal scale.

This is especially important when combining dense scores with lexical scores. Raw values may have incompatible ranges and meanings; hybrid retrieval needs an explicit fusion method.

## Query and document compatibility

### The representation contract

An embedding pipeline has a contract:

- the same or compatible model family is used for the query and indexed documents;
- the vector dimension matches the index schema;
- the metric matches the model's intended comparison behavior;
- the same normalization policy is applied at indexing and query time; and
- the model's input limits and instruction format are respected.

Breaking this contract can produce vectors that are numerically valid but semantically incomparable. A dimension mismatch may fail loudly. A model mismatch may fail silently by returning plausible but poor candidates.

### Instruction and prefix conventions

Some retrieval models expect different input prefixes or instructions for queries and passages. E5, for example, is presented as a family of text embedding models trained for retrieval and other single-vector tasks. The model's paper and documentation define how its inputs should be formatted. [Wang et al., “Text Embeddings by Weakly-Supervised Contrastive Pre-training”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03533)

The general lesson is broader than one model: an embedding model's input convention is part of the model interface. Omitting it can change the representation even when the visible text is unchanged.

### Multilingual and domain compatibility

A multilingual model may map multiple languages into a shared space, but support for a language does not imply equal retrieval quality across domains. A general model may understand everyday language while underrepresenting product codes, legal phrasing, medical abbreviations, or internal terminology.

Domain evaluation should use the questions and documents that matter for the intended system. A model that performs well on a public benchmark is not automatically the right model for a private corpus.

## Choosing an embedding model

Model selection is a compatibility and evaluation problem, not a leaderboard lookup.

### Important dimensions

Consider:

- **input limits:** how much text can be represented in one call;
- **vector dimension:** storage and index cost, but not a direct measure of quality;
- **language coverage:** whether the corpus and queries are represented well;
- **domain vocabulary:** identifiers, abbreviations, and specialized terms;
- **query/document behavior:** whether the model is trained for retrieval;
- **metric and normalization:** how vectors should be compared;
- **latency and cost:** how indexing and query-time computation fit the system; and
- **deployment and privacy:** where text is sent and how the model is operated.

Representative families include sentence-transformer models, BGE, E5, Voyage, and hosted embedding APIs. These are examples of model categories, not a universal ranking. SBERT demonstrated a siamese approach for producing sentence embeddings that can be compared with cosine similarity. [Reimers and Gurevych, “Sentence-BERT”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084)

### Evaluate retrieval, not just embedding geometry

An embedding model can produce visually smooth clusters or high similarity for paraphrases and still retrieve the wrong evidence for the target task. Evaluation should include:

- representative questions;
- relevant chunk or document labels;
- exact identifiers and rare terms;
- paraphrased questions;
- ambiguous questions;
- different languages if relevant;
- unanswerable questions; and
- version-sensitive or conflicting documents.

Measure candidate recall and ranking quality, then inspect failures. The model is only one part of the retrieval system: chunk boundaries, metadata filters, index approximation, and query processing also influence results.

### Stale embeddings

If a source changes, the vector created from the old text is stale. If only some chunks are updated, the index may contain multiple versions of the same logical document. Retrieval can then surface obsolete evidence unless version and effective-date metadata are used.

### Re-embedding

Changing an embedding model or its preprocessing usually requires re-embedding the affected corpus. The new vectors may have a different dimension, geometry, score distribution, or semantic behavior. They should not be mixed casually with vectors from the old model in one comparison space.

A safe conceptual migration keeps old and new indexes distinguishable, evaluates the new representation on a fixed test set, and switches deliberately. During a transition, source IDs and chunk IDs remain the bridge between the two indexes.

### Dimensionality and compression

More dimensions can provide more representational capacity, but dimension count alone does not establish quality. It increases storage and can affect index construction and search cost. Vector compression can reduce cost later, but it introduces approximation error and belongs to the index/storage design as well as the embedding design.

## What embeddings do not guarantee

Embeddings do not guarantee:

- factual correctness of the input;
- retrieval of every relevant passage;
- exact identifier matching;
- authorization enforcement;
- preservation of every table or code relationship;
- compatibility with a different model's vectors; or
- a grounded final answer.

Semantic similarity is also not identical to relevance. A passage can be about the same topic but fail to answer the question. The retrieval stage therefore needs explicit evaluation and often complementary signals such as sparse search, metadata filters, or reranking.

Embeddings are one transformation in the larger pipeline described earlier: preparation produces text, the encoder turns it into a vector, and a compatible index makes it searchable. That transformation does not replace preparation, retrieval evaluation, context construction, or answer verification.

Once document and query vectors share a compatible representation, the system still needs a structure that stores their records and finds candidate neighbors. That is the role of vector stores and indexes in the next chapter.
